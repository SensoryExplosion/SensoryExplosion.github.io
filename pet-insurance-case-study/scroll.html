Pet insurance reimagined

Designing a native solution based on insights from behavioural science

The case for change

Understanding user behaviour

We teamed up with a consulting firm specializing in behavioural science to get a better sense of user behavior and the way people engage with insurance products. The research highlighted the cognitive biases that shape user decisions, guiding our efforts to transform abstract insurance concepts into relatable, user-friendly experiences. I followed the insights I’ve gained throughout the project.

Designing a native solution

Our current webview solution limits native functionality - gestures, deeplinking, and animations don’t perform as users expect. The webview also uses different components than the native app, creating a disjointed look and feel. My goal was to design a fully native flow following the George Design System, creating new components that align with its guidelines.

Finance is highly regulated. The language we choose must balance strict legal requirements with human understanding. That’s why copy plays a central role in financial product design.

Heart over function

The product page is our opportunity to spark interest by presenting both the product’s details and its value. Legal requirements, along with the tendencies of banks and insurers to emphasize functional benefits over emotional values, result in a dense, text-heavy screen that feels distant and overwhelming from the first moment. In an attempt to make this screen more inviting, I used techniques from our behavioral science research, like framing, chunking, and social proofing, to turn a sea of information into a relatable experience.

Barking up the right tree

Getting insurance is part of an unconscious process, and cognitive biases play a great role in that. Framing information is a very critical part: people consider a risk much more than an advantage. By focusing on the risks first while also providing a solution, we make our case more convincing and relevant than if we were simply to list the things our product covers.

Value before variables

When dealing with information-heavy products like insurance, the simplest words are often the most effective. By making the benefits more relatable and less abstract, we can keep the user focused on the value rather than immediately jumping straight to cost and limit comparisons. This approach is especially effective in the early stages, where emotional buy-in is essential.

The power of stories

While social proofing can build trust and we can influence the behaviour of someone by showing them how others have acted, the goal of including testimonials was to close the gap between something abstract and real. Insurance products are abstract things by nature. Lining up benefits, covers, and pricing won’t help customers grasp the full value. Through unique stories, testimonials show that accidents do happen - and that the product has already made a difference for others when it mattered most.

Paw after paw

Screens overloaded with input fields often cause users to abandon the process. By splitting pet details across multiple screens, we ease this burden and create a smoother, more manageable flow. This approach taps into the Spark Effect: starting with simple actions encourages users to keep going. Capturing the pet’s name early lets us personalize later steps, using it to build a sense of involvement and connection that enhances perceived value.

Bark yes to consent

Balancing legal requirements with user experience is no small feat in the banking world. Our behavioural research identified dense legal language as a key source of high cognitive load, leading to higher drop-off rates and missed details about insurance products. By focusing on clear copy and logical design, my goal was to create a flow that respects regulatory requirements without overwhelming users, helping them understand the ‘ins and outs' of their insurance before making a decision.

Clear copy, informed choices

Legal consents are often filled with industry-specific language that can overwhelm users, making it harder for them to engage with essential information. Research shows that too much information can actually reduce transparency, as users tend to skip over details they don’t understand. With friendly copy and icons, we can clearly communicate the purpose of each document and can encourage users to make informed choices before insuring their pet.

From toggle-maze to a tap

Our current consent screen presents a maze of toggle switches, with each consent requiring individual interaction. While this design is suitable for scenarios allowing selective consent, it's less appropriate in our “take it or leave it” situation where users must agree to all terms in order to insure their pet. Going from multiple toggles to a single confirmation cell better fits our scenario; it reduces interaction cost without compromising on compliance.

Thanks for scrolling!

I hope this approach shows that with the right design, even insurance products with complex legal requirements can roll over and play nice with UX.
