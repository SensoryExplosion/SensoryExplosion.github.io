Designing a digital identity app with AI in the loop

A concept for making credentials, consent and data sharing easier to understand while exploring AI across the product design process.

  • UX/UI Design

Contents

    This project is an exploration of how the AI tools available today can assist the product design process. I used a range of tools and methods to help me carry out research, create user flows and wireframes, and produce high-fidelity designs.

    I chose the European Digital Identity Framework as my test subject in order to set constraints on the project and to work on something useful.

    About the European Digital Identity Framework

    The European Digital Identity Wallet aims to provide people with a secure, interoperable way to prove who they are and to share verified information with public and private services across the EU. Instead of users having to hand over their entire identity document, they can share only the data the service needs – for example, whether they are over 18 – while keeping their other personal data private. Member States will develop their own wallets to common European standards, allowing credentials to work across borders and services.

    Article 5a(14) eIDAS

    Users shall have full control of the use of and of the data in their European Digital Identity Wallet.

    Building blocks

    I researched the regulation, large‑scale pilots, architecture and technical specifications, then used AI to synthesize the material into an evolving PRD I updated as the project developed.

    1. EU Digital Identity Regulation

      Defined the legal requirements for the wallet, including privacy, security and user controls, as well as cross-border use.

    2. Large-scale Pilots

      Revealed how the wallet behaves across real services and messy real-life contexts.

    3. Architecture and Reference Framework

      Explained how the wallet ecosystem works across issuers, users and relying parties, grounding the experience in real system interactions.

    Findings that shaped the app

    Accessibility pushed the app toward native iOS

    The pilots treated accessibility as a baseline requirement. System-level accessibility settings should carry through into the app. That makes native components the sensible default, since they inherit much of this behaviour automatically. That’s why I decided to go for a native SwiftUI iOS app.

    Digital credentials shouldn’t resemble digital cards

    Most digital identity documents contain the same data and pictograms as their physical counterparts, but they should not function like digital ID cards. In all cases, they must be validated by a reader, it is not sufficient simply to display them on a screen. I deliberately avoided card-like UI and physical ID conventions, making that distinction clear in the design.

    One presentation flow

    The pilots showed that users were often unsure which document and which QR code to present. Aligned with the ARF, the wallet therefore uses a single QR code: the relying party requests the required data, the wallet gathers it from the available documents, and the user simply reviews and approves what is shared.

    From use cases to user flows

    Based on the use cases analysed during the research, I tasked Codex with mapping out the key user journeys. In the majority of cases, her solutions were spot on. The diagrams were created using the Mermaid Chart plugin, which I was able to easily modify using natural language whenever a node needed adjusting.

    Exploring reusable UI patterns

    With the flows in place, I used the Ideate skill from Codex’s Product Design plugin to quickly generate wireframes to test out different layouts and patterns.

    The aim was to explore a small set of recurring patterns that could be reused across the app and easily adapted to native iOS components.

    Codex initially struggled with low-fidelity wireframes. Mentioning iOS often pushed the output toward polished glass materials with heavy styling, when I was still trying to explore patterns. To get the model under control, I eventually created a simple Figma reference, along with a DESIGN.md file containing design guidelines, which resulted in much simpler and more accurate iterations. It was perfect for prototypes, but because of inconsistencies if I wanted to use them as the basis for the final designs, I had to finalise them in Figma.

    The final experience in SwiftUI

    I created the final high-fidelity design using SwiftUI and iOS 27 components based on the wireframes. I relied on the Figma MCP, a UI to JSON plugin and screenshots to provide context for Codex.

    Codex handled standard native components very well and stayed close to Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines. Custom compositions were harder: components built from several native elements often drifted visually or structurally. Manually looking up the exact API from the Developer Documentation and showing Codex reference screenshots from other native iOS apps usually got the implementation back on track.

    I created the app icon in Figma and Icon Composer. For in-app icons, I used the Lucide open-source icon library and SF Symbols when animation and a native look were needed.

    Tip Spinning up an in-app simulator stream within Codex lets you annotate exact details on screens so you don’t need to take screenshots or over-explain a change request in detail.

    A couple of lessons

    About AI

    I enjoy using AI to brainstorm, help me with research, draft outlines or draw diagrams. Using AI to help me think through problems, quickly try out solutions and iterate is lovely and the models really speed things up. The final output, visual judgment, consistency and knowing when something simply felt wrong still demanded the most human attention. In many cases, prompting my way through polish was tiresome and frustrating. Nevertheless, I like to look at things from the perspective of potential: if a model is capable of doing something today, even at a basic level, it will be able to do it perfectly in the future.

    Writing followed the same pattern. I love the saying “Every first draft is perfect, because all a first draft has to do is exist,” and in that sense AI is useful because I don’t have to stare at a blank page, but without careful review and editing, it still feels off.

    Model choice also became part of the workflow. The weekly usage limit can be hit very quickly. Choosing the right model and effort is a valuable skill in this regard. At the time of writing, I use GPT-5.6 Sol High to plan and reason through more complex tasks, then hand most of the execution over to GPT-5.6 Luna Extra High. At the moment, this is the best intelligence-to-usage ratio that has worked for me.

    About the European Digital Identity Framework

    I have a positive outlook in that, in many cases, it would be useful if we had common European cross-border standards and did not outsource the processing of our data to US tech companies. Recently, I’ve actually come across two extreme cases while travelling. In digitally advanced Norway, I had to upload my travel documents to a hotel management system when booking accommodation, whilst in the digitally less advanced Spain, we had to send photos of our ID via WhatsApp. Now, the former is obviously more credible than the latter, but honestly, I wouldn’t want to share my official documents with any US tech company. Therefore, a common European infrastructure would be welcome.

    The harder challenge is adoption. A wallet can be technically secure and standards-compliant and still fail if people do not trust the institutions behind it.
